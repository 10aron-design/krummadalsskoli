1.

The Price Is a Conversation

Supply · Demand · Equilibrium
Passed

Nobody sets prices. Everybody does.

A price is not decided by one person in a back room. It is the meeting point of two opposing forces. Demand is everyone who wants the thing: the cheaper it gets, the more of them show up. Supply is everyone selling the thing: the more it pays, the more of it they produce.

Where the two lines cross is the equilibrium: the one price where the amount people want to buy exactly matches the amount sellers want to sell. Above it, surpluses pile up. Below it, shelves empty. The market keeps drifting back to the crossing point on its own, like water finding its level.

If a curve itself moves (not just the price), economists say it "shifts". That is what you control below.

2.

Money Is Always Melting

Inflation · Purchasing Power · Real vs Nominal
Passed

The 1.000 kr note that quietly shrinks

Inflation means prices in general rise over time, so each króna buys a little less than it did last year. A 3% rate sounds harmless, but inflation compounds: each year's increase is calculated on top of the last one, the same way interest snowballs in a bank account.

This is why economists separate nominal values (the number printed on the price tag) from real values (what you can actually buy). Your salary can rise every year while your real salary, its purchasing power, stands still or falls.

Iceland knows this story. In the early 1980s inflation passed 80% a year, and in 1981 the currency was redenominated: 100 old krónur became 1 new one.

3.

Every Choice Has a Shadow

Opportunity Cost · Trade-offs · The PPF
Passed

The real cost of anything is what you gave up to get it

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you didn't choose. A free concert ticket isn't free if you skip a paid shift to use it. The shift's wages are the ticket's true price.

Whole countries face the same logic. Everything an economy could possibly produce sits on a curve called the production possibilities frontier. Making more of one thing means sliding along the curve and making less of the other. The curve bows outward: the more extreme your choice, the more each extra unit costs you.

4.

How Hard Can You Squeeze?

Price Elasticity of Demand · The Revenue Test
Passed

Some prices you can raise. Some will ruin you.

Demand always slopes downward, but not equally steeply. Price elasticity of demand measures how violently quantity reacts when price changes:

E = %Δ quantity ÷ %Δ price

If |E| > 1, demand is elastic: customers flee at the first price hike, so raising prices destroys your revenue. If |E| < 1, demand is inelastic: people buy almost the same amount no matter what, so raising prices prints money. What decides it? Whether substitutes exist, whether the good is a necessity, and how big a share of income it eats.

This is why cigarette taxes raise revenue, why insulin prices are politically explosive, and why airlines charge business travellers triple.

5.

The Anatomy of a Tax

Consumer Surplus · Producer Surplus · Deadweight Loss
Passed

Every trade creates invisible profit. Taxes burn some of it.

When you'd happily pay 2.000 kr for a kebab and it costs 1.400, the 600 kr you "saved" is consumer surplus. The seller who'd accept 1.000 and got 1.400 earns producer surplus. A free market maximizes the sum of the two. That total is the whole point of trade.

Now insert a tax. It drives a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep. Some surplus becomes government revenue, which at least gets spent on something. But the trades that the tax priced out of existence vanish entirely. That destroyed value is the deadweight loss: surplus that no one gets, not buyers, not sellers, not the state.

Watch the grey triangle below. It grows with the square of the tax: double the tax, quadruple the waste.

6.

Inside the Firm

Marginal Cost · Average Cost · Profit Maximization
Passed

Produce until the next unit stops being worth it

A firm's costs come in layers. Fixed costs (rent, machines) exist whether you produce or not. Marginal cost (MC) is what the next single unit costs you, and it usually rises as you push production: overtime pay, strained machines, rush orders.

The golden rule of production: keep producing while the price you get exceeds the marginal cost, and stop exactly where MC = price. One unit fewer leaves profit on the table. One unit more loses money on that unit. Profit per unit is the gap between price and average total cost (ATC), so total profit is that gap times quantity.

Profit = (P − ATC) × Q · maximize where MC = P

7.

The Only Game in Town

Monopoly · Market Power · Welfare Loss
Passed

A monopolist doesn't take the price. It chooses it.

In a competitive market, no single seller can move the price; everyone produces until price equals marginal cost. A monopolist faces the whole demand curve alone, so it can restrict quantity to push the price up. It deliberately sells less than the market wants in order to charge more for each unit.

The catch: raise the price too far and so many buyers walk away that profit falls again. Somewhere in the middle sits the profit-maximizing price. But notice what the monopoly destroys: every buyer who valued the product above its production cost, yet below the monopoly price, simply goes without. That unserved value is pure deadweight loss, the same grey triangle from Lesson 5, this time caused by market power instead of a tax.

Demand here: P = 100 − Q. Production cost: 20 kr per unit, flat. A competitive market would settle at P = 20, Q = 80.

8.

The Trap of Rational Players

Game Theory · Prisoner's Dilemma · Nash Equilibrium
Passed

When everyone plays smart and everyone loses

When a market has just a few firms, each one's best move depends on what the others do. Game theory studies exactly this. Its most famous game: two players each choose to cooperate (keep prices high) or defect (undercut). Both cooperate: 3 points each. Both defect: 1 point each. You defect while they cooperate: you grab 5, they get 0.

Here is the trap. Whatever your rival does, defecting pays you more. So two perfectly rational players both defect and earn 1 + 1, even though 3 + 3 was sitting right there. That outcome, where nobody can improve by changing only their own move, is a Nash equilibrium. It explains price wars, doping in sport, and why cartels keep collapsing.

But play the game repeatedly and everything changes: today's betrayal can be punished tomorrow, so cooperation becomes sustainable. The strategy below, tit-for-tat, simply copies your previous move back at you.

10 rounds against tit-for-tat. It opens by cooperating, then mirrors whatever you did last round. Try to beat 30 points.

9.

The Most Powerful Force Is Boring

GDP · Compound Growth · The Rule of 70
Passed

Two percent doesn't sound like destiny. It is.

GDP is the market value of everything a country produces in a year. What matters for living standards is not its level but its growth rate, because growth compounds exactly like inflation did in Lesson 2, only this time it works for you.

The shortcut every economist carries: the rule of 70. Divide 70 by the growth rate and you get the years it takes for the economy to double. At 2%, doubling takes 35 years. At 7%, it takes 10. Over a working lifetime, that difference separates rich countries from poor ones more than oil, geography, or luck.

Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate (%)
South Korea grew near 7–9% for three decades and went from poorer than Ghana in 1960 to one of the richest countries on Earth. Compounding did that.

10.

One Number to Steer Them All

Central Banking · Policy Rates · The Inflation Target
Passed

You are now the Governor of the Central Bank

A central bank's main lever is the policy interest rate: the price of borrowing money. Raise it and loans get expensive, spending cools, and inflation falls, but unemployment rises. Cut it and the economy heats up, hiring picks up, and prices start climbing. Every decision trades one pain against another, and the effects arrive with a lag.

Most central banks, including Seðlabanki Íslands, aim for inflation around 2.5%: low enough not to hurt, high enough to leave room to cut rates in a crisis. Your inheritance below: inflation running at 8% after a boom. Bring it to target without strangling the labour market.

Rough rule inside this simulator: inflation drifts down when your rate is well above inflation, and up when it's below. Unemployment moves the other way. You have 10 years.

Why Trade Beats Talent

Absolute vs Comparative Advantage · Specialization
Passed

The most counterintuitive result in economics

Suppose Iceland is better than Spain at producing fish and at growing tomatoes (geothermal greenhouses are no joke). Should Iceland make both and trade nothing? No, and this is the result that has surprised people for two centuries.

What matters is not who is best (absolute advantage) but who gives up less to produce something (comparative advantage). Iceland sacrifices a lot of fish for every tomato it grows; Spain sacrifices very little. So Iceland should pour everything into fish, Spain into tomatoes, and both trade. Total world output rises, and both countries end up consuming more than they could alone. Same workers, same hours, more stuff. The gain comes purely from organizing.

12.

The Price of a Country

Exchange Rates · Winners & Losers · The Króna
Passed

An exchange rate is just a price, with a country attached

The ISK/EUR rate is the price of one euro measured in krónur, set by supply and demand like everything in Course I. Who demands krónur? Tourists landing at Keflavík, foreigners buying Icelandic fish. Who supplies them? Icelanders buying PlayStations, holidays in Spain, imported flour.

Here is the part people get backwards: a weak króna is not simply bad. It makes imports painful, yes, but it makes everything Iceland sells suddenly cheap to the world: fish, aluminium, hotel rooms, flights. Exporters and the tourism industry quietly cheer when the króna slides, while everyone holding a Spotify subscription priced in euros groans. Every exchange rate movement is a transfer between these two camps.

13.

The Equation That Runs Finance

Time Value of Money · Compounding · Present Value
Passed

A króna today is worth more than a króna tomorrow

Why? Because today's króna can be invested and start growing immediately. This single idea, the time value of money, underlies every stock price, mortgage, pension, and company valuation on the planet. Future money must be discounted back to today to be compared fairly:

PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ

Flip it around and you get compounding, and with it the most expensive mistake young people make: starting late. Because growth compounds, the first years of investing matter far more than the last. Ten years of head start can beat decades of bigger contributions, as the exercise below will demonstrate at your expense.

Two savers, both investing 20.000 kr a month until age 65. One starts now. One starts later. Same money per month, wildly different endings.
